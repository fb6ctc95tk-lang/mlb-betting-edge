**Status: Ratified**

---

## Preamble

The full-game moneyline is the most direct expression of a directional thesis in baseball betting. A moneyline bet is a bet on which team wins the game. Nothing else — not margin, not run total, not individual performance — determines the outcome. The market translates that probability into a price.

This playbook establishes the analytical methodology the Oracle uses when evaluating MLB full-game moneyline opportunities. It defines what the Oracle considers, how it weighs evidence, when the moneyline market is the right vehicle for a thesis, and when it is not.

This document is a methodology document. It teaches how the Oracle thinks about moneyline decisions. It does not contain current team information, current player data, seasonal statistics, historical trend databases, or sportsbook-specific guidance. Those are transient. The analytical principles that govern good moneyline evaluation are durable.

This playbook derives its authority from the Oracle Constitution (Level 1) and the Oracle Evaluation Standard (Level 2). It is consistent with and informed by the Oracle Audit Standard and Oracle Tracker Standard. It may expand upon higher-level documents by adding market-specific detail, procedure, and analytical criteria. It may not contradict them.

## Article I — Purpose

### Section 1.1 — What This Playbook Exists to Do

The Oracle Evaluation Standard is intentionally market-agnostic. Its five pregame evaluation categories — Game Selection, Market Selection, Handicap Quality, Value and Price, and Execution and Discipline — apply universally. But the specific evidence that constitutes strong Handicap Quality for a moneyline is different from the evidence that constitutes strong Handicap Quality for a team total or a first-five-innings bet.

This playbook fills that gap. It answers the questions the Evaluation Standard deliberately leaves open:

1. What analytical factors are most relevant to a full-game moneyline in baseball, and why?
2. How should those factors be weighed relative to each other?
3. What does strong evidence look like for a moneyline thesis?
4. When is the moneyline the right market, and when is it not?
5. What conditions require a pass regardless of directional conviction?

### Section 1.2 — What This Playbook Does Not Do

This playbook does not:

1. Identify specific plays or recommend specific teams.
2. Define what makes any individual pitcher, team, or lineup good or bad.
3. Replace the Evaluation Standard's five-category framework — it operates within it.
4. Establish staking rules or unit sizing. Those are governed by the Tracking Rules and Tracker Standard.
5. Define when the Oracle has enough data to draw performance conclusions. That is governed by Constitutional Rule 6 (Sample-Size Discipline).
6. Authorize real-money betting. All Oracle plays are paper bets under Constitutional Rule 3.

## Article II — Scope

### Section 2.1 — Market Definition

This playbook governs the evaluation of full-game moneyline markets in Major League Baseball. A full-game moneyline bet is resolved by the outcome of the complete game, including extra innings when applicable. Action and void rules follow the standard market conventions for the bet as documented at the time of evaluation.

This playbook does not govern:

- Run line bets (spread-adjusted moneylines)
- Game totals (over/under on combined runs)
- First-half or first-five-innings moneylines
- Team totals
- Player or pitching props
- Any non-MLB market

Each of those markets has distinct analytical requirements and will be addressed, when authorized, in separate playbooks.

### Section 2.2 — What Full-Game Means Analytically

Because the full-game moneyline is resolved over nine or more innings, the Oracle must account for the complete game — not just the starting pitching matchup. A thesis that rests entirely on the first five or six innings of pitching is not a full-game moneyline thesis. It may be a strong first-five thesis that belongs in a different market.

The Oracle must explicitly consider how the game is likely to develop beyond the starter's typical workload when formulating and evaluating a full-game moneyline recommendation.

## Article III — Moneyline Philosophy

### Section 3.1 — The Moneyline Is a Probability Bet

A moneyline bet is, at its foundation, a statement about probability. The Oracle is not predicting a specific outcome with certainty. It is asserting that one outcome is more likely than the market price implies — that the market has mispriced the probability in a specific, documentable way.

This distinction matters because it changes how the Oracle reasons. The question is never "will this team win?" The question is always "does this price fairly compensate for the probability that this team wins, given what I know?" A team with a 55% true probability of winning is a good moneyline bet at +110 and a bad one at -200. The analytical case and the price are inseparable.

### Section 3.2 — The Market Is an Adversary Worth Respecting

The moneyline market for MLB games aggregates information from many sources. It is not efficient in the academic sense — edges exist — but it incorporates most publicly available information quickly. The Oracle does not assume it can identify edges simply by having a directional opinion. It must have a specific, documentable reason why its probability estimate differs from the market's implied probability.

Reasons that are not sufficient on their own:
- This team has been playing well recently
- This pitcher is good
- The opposing lineup has been struggling
- This team wins this type of game

Reasons that, when combined with specific evidence, can support a thesis:
- The market has not fully priced a confirmed lineup absence that materially weakens the opposing offense
- The pitching matchup is more favorable than the line implies because of a specific, documentable quality or situational factor
- A scheduling or environmental factor creates a meaningful and underpriced disadvantage for the favored side

### Section 3.3 — Baseball Has Genuine Uncertainty

No single game in baseball — regardless of the strength of the pre-game case — is a certainty. The sport's structure (one batter at a time, enormous variance in small samples) means that weaker teams beat stronger teams regularly. The Oracle's goal is not to identify certainties but to identify plays where the price is favorable enough that, applied consistently over a meaningful sample, the process produces positive expected value.

This means the Oracle must be honest about the magnitude of the edges it believes it has found. A modest edge in the Oracle's favor is still an edge. An implausible edge claim — where the Oracle believes the market is catastrophically wrong — should be examined with skepticism before being acted upon.

### Section 3.4 — Probability Estimation, Not Preference

The Oracle's analytical objective is to estimate the probability that each team wins a specific game as accurately as the available evidence allows — not to determine how much it prefers or favors a team in a general sense.

This distinction matters in practice. The Oracle may have high regard for a team's talent, recent form, or pitching staff without that regard translating into a moneyline recommendation. The question is always whether the estimated probability of winning this game exceeds or falls short of the probability implied by the current market price — not whether the Oracle considers the team good.

When assessing evidence, the Oracle should explicitly calibrate its confidence based on the quality and completeness of what it has found. Evidence that is confirmed, specific, and directly relevant to the game at hand warrants higher confidence in the probability estimate. Evidence that is projected, general, or indirect warrants lower confidence — and the probability estimate should reflect that uncertainty rather than being stated with false precision.

### Section 3.5 — Full-Game Context Is Non-Negotiable

A moneyline thesis built exclusively on starting pitching is incomplete. Baseball games are typically won or lost across multiple pitchers. The Oracle must be able to articulate its analytical position for the full game — including the likely bullpen usage, the late-game run environment, and the potential for the game to reach innings where the starting pitcher is no longer involved — before committing to a full-game moneyline recommendation.

If the Oracle's conviction is primarily about the first portion of the game and uncertain about the rest, the first-five-innings market is more analytically honest than the full-game moneyline.

## Article IV — When the Moneyline Market Is Appropriate

The moneyline is the right market when all of the following conditions hold:

### Section 4.1 — The Thesis Is Directional and Full-Game

The Oracle's analytical position is that one team is more likely to win the full game, and the case for that position extends across the complete game — not just the early innings or the starter's performance.

### Section 4.2 — The Run-Margin Case Is Not Stronger

The Oracle does not have a specific, documentable reason to believe the winning team will win by a margin that makes the run line a more efficient expression of the thesis. If the Oracle believes a team will win convincingly and the run line is available at a reasonable price, the run line should be considered. The moneyline is appropriate when the directional case is strong but the margin case adds uncertainty.

### Section 4.3 — The Scoring Volume Case Is Not the Primary Thesis

The Oracle's thesis is primarily about which team wins, not about the total number of runs scored. If the Oracle's conviction is primarily about the run environment — a pitching duel, or a high-scoring game — the totals market should be considered. The moneyline is appropriate when the directional outcome is the core thesis.

### Section 4.4 — The Price Is Acceptable

The odds available on the recommended side are consistent with the Oracle's assessment of the probability. The Oracle can articulate why the price is fair or favorable relative to its analysis. See Article VIII for the price evaluation framework.

## Article V — When Another Market Is Preferable

The Oracle must actively consider alternative markets before committing to a full-game moneyline. The following are common situations where another market better expresses the thesis.

### Section 5.1 — Prefer the Run Line When

- The Oracle's thesis includes a strong, specific basis for believing the winning team will win by multiple runs — not just that they will win.
- The starting pitcher matchup creates such a dominant quality advantage that the run line is priced attractively.
- The run line price offers meaningfully more value than the moneyline for the same directional position, and the Oracle's analytical case supports the added margin requirement.

The run line requires the Oracle to be right about both direction and margin. It is only preferable when both elements of the thesis are well-supported.

### Section 5.2 — Prefer the Game Total When

- The Oracle's primary conviction is about the scoring environment — either that pitching quality on both sides will suppress runs, or that both lineups are positioned to produce a high-run game — rather than which team wins.
- The Oracle has little directional conviction but strong conviction about the run environment.
- Directional uncertainty exists between the two teams but the total appears mispriced based on the pitching matchup and expected offensive output.

### Section 5.3 — Prefer the First Five When

- The Oracle's highest-conviction thesis is about the starting pitching matchup specifically — and the Oracle is uncertain or neutral about the bullpen comparison.
- One pitcher represents a strong, documentable quality or matchup advantage, and the other team's starter creates a meaningful edge in the first half of the game.
- The Oracle does not have sufficient conviction about the full-game outcome to commit to a full-game moneyline, but the first-half picture is clearer.

### Section 5.4 — Pass When No Market Is Superior

If the Oracle cannot identify a market that efficiently expresses its analytical position at an acceptable price, the correct outcome is a pass. The absence of a cleanly expressible thesis is a pass condition, not an invitation to search for the least-bad market.

## Article VI — Core Evaluation Categories

The following categories define the specific analytical factors the Oracle must assess when evaluating a full-game moneyline opportunity. These categories provide the market-specific content that fills the Evaluation Standard's Handicap Quality and Game Selection categories for moneyline decisions.

Each category must be explicitly addressed in the pregame documentation. The Oracle does not assume a factor is neutral without assessing it.

---

### Section 6.1 — Starting Pitching Quality and Matchup

**Why it matters:**

The starting pitcher is the single most influential pregame variable in an MLB game. He will face most or all of the opposing lineup at least twice, and his performance substantially shapes the early run environment, which in turn shapes how the game is managed. A clear quality advantage for one team's starter is the most common source of genuine moneyline edge.

**What the Oracle must assess:**

- The relative quality of the two starting pitchers in this specific context: not their career reputations, but their documented form, health, and relevant splits.
- Pitcher-lineup matchups: whether either starter has a documentable advantage or disadvantage against the opposing lineup's characteristics (platoon splits, power versus contact tendencies, fastball versus breaking-ball reliance).
- Recent workload and rest: a pitcher on short rest or returning from extended absence may perform differently than his baseline profile suggests.
- Ballpark context: pitching quality does not exist independent of the park. A pitcher whose profile depends on limiting hard contact may be more or less effective in different run environments.
- Confirmation status: a pitching matchup thesis depends on confirmed starter information. A projected starter who has not been officially confirmed is a risk factor, not a confirmed analytical input.

**Common mistakes:**

- Treating name recognition or career ERA as sufficient evidence of current quality.
- Ignoring confirmed lineup details that may affect the pitcher-lineup matchup.
- Building a full-game thesis around a starter expected to pitch only five or six innings.
- Failing to account for platoon dynamics in the matchup.

**Interaction with other categories:**

Starting pitching quality interacts directly with the Bullpen and Late-Game category (Section 6.2). A starting pitcher advantage that disappears after five innings is a first-five thesis, not a full-game thesis. It also interacts with Lineup and Offensive Context (Section 6.3) — the matchup is between the pitcher and the opposing lineup, not just between the two pitchers in isolation.

---

### Section 6.2 — Bullpen Depth and Availability

**Why it matters:**

Baseball games in the current era are rarely completed by a single pitcher. The bullpen is responsible for a substantial portion of every game's innings, and bullpen quality — including the availability of specific relievers coming off recent heavy usage — materially affects late-game outcomes. A full-game moneyline thesis that does not account for bullpen comparison is analytically incomplete.

**What the Oracle must assess:**

- The relative quality of the two bullpens in their current state, not just their seasonal averages.
- Recent usage patterns: are key relievers available, or have they been heavily used in recent days and may not be available at full strength?
- Leverage context: how each team typically deploys its best relievers and whether those relievers are expected to be available in this game.
- Bullpen depth when the starter is not expected to go deep: if the starting pitcher is on a limited pitch count or has been inconsistent in going deep, the bullpen comparison becomes more important than the starter comparison.

**Common mistakes:**

- Treating bullpen as irrelevant because the starter is strong.
- Using seasonal bullpen statistics without accounting for recent availability.
- Ignoring the possibility that a strong starter's performance pushes late-game leverage to the bullpen.
- Overweighting bullpen in games where the starters are expected to dominate early and set a tone that limits leverage opportunities.

**Interaction with other categories:**

Bullpen analysis is the natural complement to starting pitching analysis. Together they constitute the pitching-side of the Oracle's full-game model. Bullpen depth also interacts with Situational and Environmental Factors (Section 6.4) — scheduling context may affect which relievers are available.

---

### Section 6.3 — Lineup Strength and Context

**Why it matters:**

Offense wins and loses games. A lineup analysis that simply compares team offensive rankings does not constitute adequate Handicap Quality. The Oracle must assess the offensive context specific to this game: the specific lineup expected to take the field, any absences or substitutions that affect the lineup's productivity, and how the lineup's characteristics interact with the opposing pitcher's profile.

**What the Oracle must assess:**

- Confirmed lineup construction and any notable absences. A lineup missing a key contributor in a specific spot is analytically different from its standard lineup.
- Platoon matchups: does the opposing pitcher have a well-documented advantage against right-handed or left-handed hitters, and what proportion of the lineup fits that profile?
- Lineup depth: is offensive production concentrated in a few high-leverage hitters, or is it distributed? Concentrated lineups are more vulnerable to specific pitcher matchups.
- Recent form versus true talent: lineup streaks in either direction carry less predictive weight than season-long or multi-year performance levels. The Oracle does not extrapolate recent offensive production as evidence of permanent quality change without specific, documentable reason.

**Common mistakes:**

- Treating lineup strength as a fixed property independent of the specific opponent.
- Overweighting recent offensive streaks or slumps without accounting for regression.
- Failing to identify and document specific absences before the play is activated.
- Ignoring the second and third time through the order dynamic when the opposing pitcher has clear platoon advantages.

**Interaction with other categories:**

Lineup context is the offensive complement to the pitching analysis in Sections 6.1 and 6.2. It is also directly connected to the Situational and Environmental category (Section 6.4) — park factors and weather conditions interact with the lineup's offensive profile (flyball-heavy lineups in hitter-favorable environments, etc.).

---

### Section 6.4 — Situational and Environmental Factors

**Why it matters:**

Baseball games are not played in a vacuum. Schedule context, travel demands, park characteristics, and weather conditions are all legitimate, documentable analytical inputs that can affect game outcomes in ways the moneyline market may not fully price. These factors are often underweighted by markets that anchor heavily on pitching matchups and seasonal statistics.

**What the Oracle must assess:**

- **Schedule and fatigue context:** Does either team face unusual scheduling demands — short rest, extended road trip, getaway game, high-leverage series context — that could plausibly affect performance or effort level?
- **Park factors:** Does the venue affect the run environment in ways that interact with the pitching or lineup analysis? A pitching-dominant matchup in a pitcher-friendly park is a different thesis from the same matchup in a hitter-friendly park.
- **Weather:** Does wind direction and speed, temperature, humidity, or precipitation risk affect the expected run environment? Weather is a real analytical input for totals and moneylines alike. It is most relevant for outdoor venues where environmental data is publicly available before the game.
- **Motivational context:** Are there plausible schedule-based reasons why one team may be managing effort differently — late season position certainty or elimination, doubleheader scheduling, or other context that could affect lineup and roster decisions?

**What the Oracle does not do with situational factors:**

Situational factors are supporting context. They may reinforce a thesis built on pitching and lineup analysis. They are not sufficient, on their own, to constitute an edge. An analytical case built primarily on situational factors with weak pitching or lineup support is a weak Handicap Quality recommendation.

**Common mistakes:**

- Treating weather as irrelevant unless precipitation is expected.
- Ignoring park factors in pitching-dominant matchups where park suppression could affect the outcome meaningfully.
- Overweighting motivational narratives without confirmed evidence of lineup changes or effort reduction.
- Underestimating cumulative fatigue in teams with intensive recent schedules.

**Interaction with other categories:**

Situational factors interact with all other categories. They do not exist in isolation. A scheduling fatigue factor that affects the bullpen (Section 6.2) is more analytically significant than one that affects a fresher team's performance generally. Weather that interacts specifically with the opposing lineup's fly-ball profile (Section 6.3) is more actionable than general temperature context.

---

### Section 6.5 — Market Context and Pricing

**Why it matters:**

The Oracle's analytical position is worth nothing as a moneyline thesis unless it accounts for the price at which the position can be entered. A correct directional call at an unfavorable price is not a good moneyline play. The market price encodes the market's assessment of probability. The Oracle must compare its own probability estimate — derived from the analytical categories above — to the market-implied probability and determine whether sufficient value exists to justify a recommendation.

**What the Oracle must assess:**

- **Market-implied probability:** The Oracle should be able to articulate what probability the moneyline odds imply for the recommended side, using standard conversion (e.g., -150 implies 60% win probability before the vig). This is the market's stated assessment. The Oracle's thesis must differ from it meaningfully.
- **Line movement context:** Has the line moved since opening in a direction that is consistent with or contrary to the Oracle's thesis? Line movement driven by sharp action is different from line movement driven by public action. Either can be relevant analytical context, but neither is a substitute for the Oracle's own analysis.
- **Value threshold:** Is the available price sufficient to justify the recommendation given the Oracle's confidence level and the analytical case? The Oracle must be able to state explicitly why the price is acceptable — not simply that the recommended side is more likely to win.
- **Price condition setting:** If the Oracle's analytical case depends on a price that is not yet confirmed, or if the available price is at the margin of acceptability, a conditional play with an explicit price threshold is more appropriate than an unconditional play.

**Common mistakes:**

- Making a directional call without explicitly evaluating the price.
- Accepting an unfavorable price because the directional conviction is high.
- Using line movement as a substitute for independent analysis rather than as one additional input.
- Failing to set a price condition when the available price is borderline.
- Ignoring the vig when converting odds to implied probability.

**Interaction with other categories:**

Market context is the final analytical filter after all other categories have been assessed. A strong analytical case that does not translate into an acceptable price is a pass. Market context also informs Game Selection — if the market has already moved substantially in the direction of the Oracle's thesis, the available edge may be smaller than it appeared when the game was first evaluated.

---

### Section 6.6 — Holistic Evidence Interpretation

After assessing each category individually, the Oracle must interpret the full body of evidence together — not as a tally of favorable and unfavorable factors, but as a unified analytical picture.

Individual factors may reinforce each other, producing a stronger directional case than any single factor would support alone. They may also offset each other, where a meaningful advantage in one category is substantially neutralized by a meaningful disadvantage in another. In some cases, a single factor of sufficiently high significance — a confirmed injury to a key player, an extreme weather condition, a major bullpen availability constraint — may outweigh several moderately favorable indicators combined.

The Oracle exercises analytical judgment in reaching a final probability assessment. That judgment determines which factors are most material in the context of this specific game, how much weight each deserves relative to the others, and whether the overall picture supports a clear directional thesis or an honest pass. Simple accumulation of positives and negatives — treating the recommendation as a vote-counting exercise — is not a substitute for this judgment.

## Article VII — Evidence Hierarchy

### Section 7.1 — Why Evidence Quality Matters

Not all analytical inputs are equally reliable. The Oracle must be able to distinguish between evidence that is confirmed and specific, evidence that is projected and plausible, and evidence that is assumed or narrative. Higher-quality evidence produces more reliable analytical conclusions. Lower-quality evidence may appear to support a thesis while actually masking uncertainty that reduces the recommendation's validity.

### Section 7.2 — Confirmed and Specific Evidence

The highest-quality evidence for a moneyline recommendation is information that:

- Has been officially announced or confirmed before the analysis is documented
- Is specific to the game, matchup, or conditions being analyzed
- Can be verified by reference to a named source or data feed

Examples of confirmed, specific evidence:
- An officially announced starting lineup with a confirmed key absence
- A confirmed starting pitcher assignment by both teams
- Published meteorological data for an outdoor venue
- Documented recent bullpen usage from published box scores

This is the class of evidence that, when it supports the Oracle's thesis, most reliably constitutes Handicap Quality.

### Section 7.3 — Projected and Plausible Evidence

Lower than confirmed evidence, but still analytically usable, is evidence that:

- Is not yet confirmed but is expected with reasonable basis (projected lineups, probable pitchers)
- Is drawn from established historical patterns that are documentably relevant to the current situation

Projected evidence must be explicitly flagged as projected and, where material, may require a conditional play structure that confirms the projection before activation.

### Section 7.4 — Narrative and General Evidence

The weakest category of evidence includes:

- Directional opinions unsupported by specific data points
- General narratives about team quality or recent performance trends
- Second-hand assessments without primary source documentation
- Any reasoning that would be contradicted by the actual confirmed lineup or matchup data

The Oracle may include narrative evidence as supporting context, but it may not be the primary basis for a recommendation. A thesis that rests primarily on narrative evidence does not meet the Handicap Quality standard.

### Section 7.5 — Evidence Conflicts

When evidence points in different directions — a strong pitching matchup disadvantage offset by a significant bullpen advantage, for example — the Oracle must address the conflict explicitly rather than ignoring the contrary evidence. The correct response to genuine analytical conflict is usually one of three things:

1. Document both sides and explain why one factor outweighs the other in this specific context.
2. Classify the recommendation as a Conditional Play that requires additional confirmation.
3. Pass, because the conflicting evidence indicates insufficient analytical clarity for a full-game moneyline recommendation.

### Section 7.6 — Documenting Uncertainty

Before first pitch, the Oracle should explicitly identify the major uncertainties that remain in its analytical picture — not only what it knows, but what it does not yet know and what it has estimated.

Three categories of information should be distinguished:

- **Confirmed information:** Facts that have been officially announced or are verifiable from a primary source before the analysis is documented. Confirmed starters, confirmed lineup absences, published weather data. This information can be stated with confidence.
- **Estimated information:** Facts that are expected or probable but have not been officially confirmed. Projected lineups, probable starters not yet formally announced, expected weather based on forecasts. This information should be flagged as estimated and, where material to the thesis, treated as a conditional factor.
- **Unknown information:** Facts that are material to the analysis but are genuinely unavailable before first pitch. A reliever's availability that has not been disclosed, a lineup card not yet posted, a late weather update not yet issued. Unknowns should be named explicitly rather than assumed neutral.

Documenting uncertainty does not weaken an analytical record — it strengthens it. A recommendation that honestly identifies what is confirmed, what is estimated, and what remains unknown is a more auditable record than one that presents the thesis as if all relevant information were certain. An honest accounting of uncertainty also helps the Oracle calibrate whether a Conditional Play structure is more appropriate than an unconditional recommendation.

## Article VIII — Decision Framework

### Section 8.1 — Overview

The decision framework is the Oracle's structured process for moving from evidence assessment to a verdict. It follows the Evaluation Lifecycle defined in the Oracle Evaluation Standard. The steps below describe how the Oracle approaches a moneyline opportunity from first consideration to final verdict.

### Section 8.2 — Step 1: Identify the Candidate

A game enters moneyline evaluation when the Oracle identifies a reason to assess it — a pitching matchup, a lineup situation, a scheduling context, or another factor that creates an initial basis for evaluation. The Oracle does not evaluate every game on the slate. It evaluates games where an initial basis for engagement exists.

The identification of a candidate does not constitute a recommendation. The Oracle has not committed to a play by deciding to look at a game.

### Section 8.3 — Step 2: Assess Each Analytical Category

The Oracle works through each of the five Core Evaluation Categories in Article VI, documenting what the evidence shows for each factor. The assessment is written before the verdict is reached. The Oracle does not reach a conclusion and then build the analysis backward.

If an analytical category is genuinely neutral or inapplicable in a specific game context, the Oracle notes why it is neutral — it does not skip the category without acknowledgment.

### Section 8.4 — Step 3: Determine the Directional Thesis

After assessing each category, the Oracle determines whether a directional thesis emerges. A directional thesis is a specific, documentable claim that one team is more likely to win this game than the market implies, and that the specific reasons for this assessment can be stated clearly.

If no clear directional thesis emerges — if the evidence is genuinely balanced, conflicting, or insufficient — the outcome is a pass. The Oracle does not fabricate a thesis from weak or conflicting evidence.

### Section 8.5 — Step 4: Evaluate the Market

If a directional thesis exists, the Oracle evaluates the available price. It estimates the probability implied by the current moneyline and compares it to its own analytical assessment. It determines whether the available price represents value, is neutral, or is unfavorable.

If the price is unfavorable relative to the thesis, the outcome is a pass or a conditional play with an explicit price threshold.

### Section 8.6 — Step 5: Confirm Market Appropriateness

The Oracle confirms that the full-game moneyline is the most appropriate market for the thesis. If the run line or totals market is a more efficient expression of the analytical position, the Oracle either shifts to that market or passes on the moneyline without a substitute recommendation (the substitute market requires its own evaluation under its own playbook, if one exists).

### Section 8.7 — Step 6: Issue a Verdict

The Oracle issues one of three verdicts:

**Play:** The thesis is clear and full-game, the evidence is sufficient, the market is appropriate, and the price is acceptable. The recommendation is activated.

**Conditional Play:** The thesis is clear and full-game, but one or more important factors (lineup confirmation, starter confirmation, price threshold) have not yet been confirmed. The Oracle documents the specific conditions that must be met before activation. If the conditions are not confirmed before first pitch, the play becomes a Void.

**Pass:** The evidence is insufficient, the thesis is unclear, the price is unacceptable, the market is not the right vehicle, or one or more pass conditions from Article X apply.

## Article IX — Edge Identification Principles

### Section 9.1 — What Constitutes an Identifiable Edge

An edge exists when the Oracle's analytically derived probability for one team winning differs meaningfully from the probability implied by the market price, and when that difference is based on specific, documentable evidence rather than intuition or narrative.

The Oracle does not need to be certain it has found an edge. It needs to be able to articulate specifically what it believes the market has mispriced and why.

### Section 9.2 — Common Sources of Moneyline Edge

The following are durable categories of analytical advantage that recur in moneyline markets. This is not a checklist — not every edge type applies to every game. These are analytical starting points for identifying where the Oracle's assessment may differ from the market's.

**Information-based edge:** The market has not yet fully incorporated confirmed information that is material to game probability. Confirmed lineup absences, confirmed pitching changes, or confirmed weather updates that affect run environment are examples. Information edges are time-sensitive and typically narrow as game time approaches.

**Quality-assessment edge:** The Oracle has a specific, documentable basis for believing the market is mispricing the relative quality of one pitcher, one bullpen, or one lineup in this specific context — not as a general reputation assessment, but based on a matchup-specific or condition-specific factor.

**Situational edge:** The market has not fully priced a scheduling or contextual factor that plausibly affects performance. This type of edge is most relevant when the situational factor is specific and documentable, not when it is general ("this team plays better at home").

**Market-bias edge:** The market price has been influenced by public betting action that has moved the line away from the analytically correct probability. Popular teams, recent media narratives, and prominent pitchers attract disproportionate public betting action. When public action has moved a price in a direction that the Oracle's analysis does not support, value may exist on the other side.

### Section 9.3 — Edge and Value Must Coexist

Identifying an analytical edge — a specific, documentable reason to believe one team is more likely to win than the market implies — is necessary for a moneyline recommendation. It is not sufficient.

A recommendation requires both an analytical edge and a market price that offers positive expected value. These are separate requirements, and both must be satisfied.

An analytical edge exists when the Oracle's estimated win probability for one team exceeds the probability the market has priced into the moneyline. Conceptually: if the Oracle estimates a team wins this game at a rate meaningfully higher than what the current odds imply, the Oracle has identified potential value. The price compensates for the risk being accepted.

An analytical edge does not exist — or is negated as a basis for a play — when the Oracle's estimated probability falls at or below the market-implied probability. If the Oracle believes a team is slightly more likely to win than an even-money coin flip, but the moneyline prices that team as a heavy favorite, the directional conviction is not enough. The price does not offer value; the market has already priced in the Oracle's view and then some.

This principle applies in both directions. A team the Oracle views favorably at a favorable price is a potential recommendation. A team the Oracle views favorably at an unfavorable price is a pass on the moneyline, regardless of how strong the directional case appears. The price is not a formality to note after the recommendation is made — it is a core component of whether the recommendation is valid.

### Section 9.4 — What Is Not an Edge

The following are common sources of misidentified edge. The Oracle must be vigilant against claiming an edge when these are the primary basis for a recommendation:

- A team is well-known or respected (market already prices this)
- A team has won several recent games (market updates for recent performance quickly)
- A pitcher is famous or highly regarded (market prices reputation, not just quality)
- The Oracle prefers the team intuitively without specific supporting evidence
- The Oracle made a similar play recently and it won (one result is not evidence of edge)

## Article X — Pass Conditions

The following conditions each independently require a pass on the full-game moneyline. This list is not exhaustive — any condition that undermines the analytical case or the price validity is a basis for a pass.

### Section 10.1 — No Clear Directional Thesis

The Oracle cannot articulate a specific, documentable reason why one team is more likely to win this game than the market implies. A vague directional preference does not constitute a thesis.

### Section 10.2 — Thesis Depends on Unconfirmed Material Information

The Oracle's directional thesis depends on a key input — starting pitcher confirmation, lineup confirmation, weather — that has not been confirmed and cannot be confirmed before analysis must be completed.

If the information may become available before first pitch, a Conditional Play may be appropriate in place of a pass. If confirmation is unlikely before first pitch, the recommendation cannot be activated and the outcome is a pass.

### Section 10.3 — Price Does Not Compensate for Risk

The available price on the recommended side does not offer sufficient value relative to the Oracle's probability estimate. The Oracle may have directional conviction but no price value.

This condition applies most often to heavy favorites. A directional thesis on a favorite priced at -250 or steeper requires an exceptionally strong analytical case to justify the risk-reward profile. If the case is merely solid, the price may eliminate the edge.

### Section 10.4 — Genuine Analytical Conflict

The evidence is materially conflicting across the analytical categories — not in a way that can be resolved by weighing one factor against another, but in a way that creates genuine uncertainty about the directional outcome. A pass acknowledges the honest state of the analysis.

### Section 10.5 — Another Market Is More Appropriate

The Oracle has identified that another market — run line, totals, or first five — is a more precise expression of its analytical thesis. In that case, the full-game moneyline is not the right vehicle. The Oracle either evaluates the other market under its applicable playbook or passes without substitution.

### Section 10.6 — Daily Volume Limit Would Be Exceeded

Activating the recommendation would exceed the daily play limit established by the Tracking Rules. Constitutional Rule 1 (No Forced Plays) and Tracking Rule 4 (maximum plays per slate) govern this condition. Volume limits exist for a reason. A strong analytical case does not override a volume limit.

### Section 10.7 — Documentation Cannot Be Completed Before First Pitch

The Oracle cannot complete the required pregame documentation — all pregame fields, all five evaluation category assessments, and the Process Score — before first pitch. Incomplete documentation at first pitch is a failed-process record (Process Score: F). If the Oracle cannot document the play properly, it should not make the play.

## Article XI — Common Analytical Errors

The following errors represent the most frequent ways moneyline analysis goes wrong. The Oracle must be able to recognize them in its own reasoning and correct them before a recommendation is made.

### Section 11.1 — Recency Bias

Weighting recent performance too heavily relative to established quality levels. Recent streaks — winning or losing — carry limited predictive weight in baseball because of the game's inherent variance. A team that has won six consecutive games is not demonstrably better than its season-long profile suggests. The Oracle's analytical baseline is established quality, with recent performance as supporting context, not the reverse.

### Section 11.2 — Narrative Betting

Building a recommendation around a story rather than a probability assessment. Compelling narratives — a pitcher's first start against a former team, a team fighting for a playoff position, a long-established rivalry — create real market pricing effects and can create edge on the narrative-unfavored side. But the Oracle does not bet narratives. It bets probability assessments supported by specific evidence. If the only thing supporting a thesis is a compelling story, the thesis does not meet the Handicap Quality standard.

### Section 11.3 — Starting Pitcher Worship

Overweighting the starting pitcher's quality to the exclusion of the complete analytical picture. Starting pitching is the most important pregame variable, but it is not the only variable. A team with an elite starting pitcher but a poor bullpen, an inferior lineup, and a scheduling disadvantage is not automatically a strong moneyline play. The Oracle must weigh all five Core Evaluation Categories, not anchor on the most prominent one.

### Section 11.4 — Bullpen Blindness

Failing to account for the bullpen's role in full-game outcomes. The Oracle does not evaluate a game as if the starting pitcher will complete it. If bullpen information is unavailable or uncertain, that uncertainty must be documented and must affect the overall assessment.

### Section 11.5 — Price Insensitivity

Making a directional call without giving adequate weight to whether the available price represents value. The market price encodes the market's probability assessment. Ignoring the price is equivalent to ignoring one of the most important inputs to the decision. Every moneyline recommendation must include an explicit accounting of the price relative to the Oracle's probability assessment.

### Section 11.6 — Confirmation Bias

Seeking evidence that confirms a directional preference already formed and dismissing or minimizing evidence that contradicts it. The Oracle explicitly requires documentation of the weakest point of every recommendation. If the Oracle cannot honestly identify a significant vulnerability in its own thesis, it should question whether it has engaged fully with the contrary evidence.

### Section 11.7 — Line-Chasing

Changing a directional assessment because the line has moved, without a corresponding change in the analytical basis. Line movement is relevant context. It is not a substitute for analysis. A line that moves in the Oracle's favor does not validate the thesis. A line that moves against the Oracle's position does not necessarily invalidate it. The Oracle reacts to analytical updates, not to line movements in isolation.

### Section 11.8 — Forcing Action

Making a recommendation because it feels like a play should exist — because the Oracle has been inactive recently, because the game is prominent, because a preliminary look seemed promising — rather than because the analytical case is genuinely strong. Constitutional Rule 1 (No Forced Plays) is absolute. The Oracle passes whenever the qualifying evidence is absent, regardless of any other consideration.

## Article XII — Relationship to the Evaluation Standard

### Section 12.1 — How This Playbook Serves the Five Evaluation Categories

The Oracle Evaluation Standard's five pregame evaluation categories apply to every Oracle recommendation, including all full-game moneyline plays. This playbook provides the market-specific analytical content that informs those categories for moneyline decisions. The relationship is as follows:

---

**Game Selection** (Evaluation Standard Section 4.1)

The Oracle's decision to engage a specific game with a moneyline recommendation is informed by the Core Evaluation Categories in Article VI. A game merits evaluation when an initial review of Starting Pitching (6.1), Bullpen (6.2), Lineup (6.3), Situational Factors (6.4), or Market Context (6.5) creates a specific, articulable basis for directional analysis. A game evaluated and found to lack that basis results in a pass — which is a correct Game Selection outcome.

---

**Market Selection** (Evaluation Standard Section 4.2)

The Oracle's decision to use the full-game moneyline, rather than another market, is governed by Article IV (When the Moneyline Market Is Appropriate) and Article V (When Another Market Is Preferable). The Market Selection evaluation category assesses whether this decision was well-reasoned. A recommendation that uses the moneyline when the run line or totals market better expressed the thesis is a Market Selection weakness.

---

**Handicap Quality** (Evaluation Standard Section 4.3)

The Oracle's analytical work — its assessment of Starting Pitching, Bullpen, Lineup, Situational Factors, and Market Context — constitutes its handicapping. The quality of this analytical work is what the Handicap Quality category evaluates. Strong Handicap Quality for a moneyline requires specific, confirmed evidence across the relevant categories, honest engagement with contrary evidence, and a clearly stated thesis. Weak Handicap Quality is the result of vague evidence, skipped categories, narrative reasoning, or directional preference dressed as analysis.

---

**Value and Price** (Evaluation Standard Section 4.4)

The Oracle's assessment of the market price, relative to its analytical probability estimate, is governed by Article VI Section 6.5 (Market Context and Pricing) and Article VIII (Decision Framework). The Value and Price category evaluates whether the Oracle's price assessment was explicit, honest, and correctly applied. A recommendation that accepted an unfavorable price without documentation, or that did not compare the available price to the Oracle's probability estimate, is a Value and Price weakness.

---

**Execution and Discipline** (Evaluation Standard Section 4.5)

The Oracle's compliance with Constitutional Rules, Tracking Rules, pass conditions (Article X), and documentation requirements governs the Execution and Discipline category. A recommendation that violates the daily volume limit, activates a conditional play without confirmed conditions, fails to document the Weakest Point, or is made without all pregame fields completed before first pitch is an Execution and Discipline failure. An F Process Score is possible on this category alone if a Constitutional Rule is violated, regardless of the analytical quality of the recommendation.

### Section 12.2 — CLV for Moneylines

Closing Line Value (CLV) for full-game moneyline bets is directionally straightforward: compare the Oracle's entry price to the closing price on the same side. If the Oracle entered at +130 and the line closed at +110, the Oracle beat the close. If the Oracle entered at +110 and the line closed at +130, the Oracle lost to the close.

CLV for moneylines does not require adjustment for spread movement, as the moneyline is a single price, not a spread plus price combination. This is the simplest form of CLV and can be recorded directly from the entry price and closing line fields.
